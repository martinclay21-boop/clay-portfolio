# Product

## Register

brand

## Platform

web

## Users

Two audiences view the same portfolio, and it reshapes for each. The primary audience is recruiters and hiring managers: time-pressed, skimming many candidates, deciding in seconds whether Clay is worth a conversation. They want the highlights, the proof, and a fast way to make contact. The secondary audience is fellow designers and design leads evaluating craft, who want the process, the decisions, and the messy middle behind the work. The site reads intent at entry (a "who are you?" gate) and adapts its content, order, and emphasis to whichever is reading.

## Product Purpose

This is Clay Martin's portfolio, built to land his first full-time design role — UX designer and researcher, with an emerging interest in product and Agile project management. Its job is to convert a cold visitor into a warm contact. Success is a recruiter reaching out. Beyond listing projects, the site is itself an argument for his ability: the way it adapts to the viewer is a working demonstration of the UX thinking it claims.

## Positioning

The portfolio designs for the person reading it, recruiter or designer, and in doing so demonstrates the exact skill it is selling: understanding a user's real need and shaping the experience around it. The medium is the proof.

## Conversion & proof

- Primary CTA is to email Clay. Résumé download and case studies are supporting proof that funnel toward that email; they are the secondary path for a visitor not ready to reach out yet.
- The line a visitor should remember after ten seconds: a UX designer and researcher who gets the problem right, then designs and tests until it works.
- Belief ladder — what a recruiter must believe, in order, before emailing: first, that this is a real designer with a point of view, not a generic template; second, that the work is grounded in genuine research and iteration, not just polished screens; third, that he is detail-obsessed and easy to work with, the "glue" a team wants; fourth, that he fits what they are hiring for. Every surface should move the reader one rung up.
- Proof on hand: five real (anonymous) peer testimonials from a group design sprint, now shown in a "What my teammates say" section, several directly backing the "glue / connective tissue" and "subtle leader" framing; six end-to-end case studies from research to tested high-fidelity prototypes; two design internships (Damar Staffing, Spokenote); ICAgile and IRB Human-Subjects certifications. The remaining stretch is a named, attributable testimonial rather than an anonymous one.

## Brand Personality

Warm, thoughtful, dependable. The voice is plain-spoken and reflective, friendly but serious, written in Clay's own cadence rather than portfolio boilerplate. It should read as a real person who works methodically through research, the real problem, and iteration, and who leads quietly rather than loudly. The feeling to evoke is trust: a hiring manager should come away sensing both the rigor of the work and the person behind it.

## Anti-references

It must not look generic or AI-made — no could-be-anyone template with no point of view. And it must not read as corporate or stiff — no jargon, LinkedIn-speak, or over-formality that flattens the human. (An earlier concept, a walkable 3D city, was built and deliberately abandoned for reading as gimmicky and unfinished; spectacle for its own sake is off the table, and any wow must come from substance.)

## Design Principles

- Understand the problem first. Every screen solves the visitor's actual need (a recruiter's need to assess fast, a designer's need for depth) rather than decorating around it.
- Show the skill by using it. The experience itself is the case study; adapting to the viewer is the argument. Practice what you preach.
- Warm, not corporate; serious, not casual. Hold the friendly-yet-serious line in copy and tone. Human voice, real substance, no buzzwords.
- Substance over spectacle. Wow comes from thoughtful craft and genuine adaptation, never from effects that distract from the work.
- Be a UX exemplar. A UX portfolio is judged as a piece of UX, so it must practice the accessibility and clarity it preaches.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Hold to WCAG 2.1 AA. Body text at 4.5:1 contrast or better (large text 3:1), full keyboard operability, a skip-to-content link, visible focus, semantic landmarks and a clean heading order, and a reduced-motion alternative for every animation. These are already in place and should stay green as the site evolves.
